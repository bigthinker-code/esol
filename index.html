Try the predictor

Type a SMILES string or pick an example. The app draws the structure, shows the prediction on a ruler, and lists which properties help or hurt solubility. It also has a batch mode for whole spreadsheets.

From a line of text to a solubility

Four steps happen between pressing Enter and seeing the result.

  1. 1

    Read the structure

    A SMILES string such as CCO describes a molecule in plain text. RDKit turns it into a molecular graph.

  2. 2

    Describe it in numbers

    Eight descriptors capture size, oiliness, polarity and flexibility. A 2048-bit fingerprint records which small fragments are present.

  3. 3

    Let the forest vote

    A Random Forest is many decision trees. Each makes its own guess and the average is the prediction. Their spread shows how sure the model is.

  4. 4

    Explain it

    logS becomes mol/L and mg/mL, gets a plain verdict, and the app lists which properties push solubility up or down.

What a logS number means

logS is the base-10 logarithm of solubility in mol/L. A logS of −2 is 0.01 mol/L and −4 is 0.0001 mol/L, so every step down is ten times less soluble. The bands below are rules of thumb, and the examples are Esol’s predictions.

−1 or higher

Highly soluble

About 0.1 mol/L or more dissolves with little effort.

Glucose, ethanol

−1 to −3

Soluble

Dissolves at normal lab concentrations.

Aspirin, caffeine, paracetamol, benzene

−3 to −5

Poorly soluble

Only small amounts dissolve. Drug work often needs formulation help here.

Ibuprofen, naphthalene

Below −5

Very poorly soluble

Almost nothing dissolves. Expect a solid or an oil.

Anthracene, cholesterol

Small organic molecules only

Salts, metals, polymers and very large molecules fall outside what the model learned.

No temperature or pH

Real solubility changes with conditions and crystal form. The model only sees the structure.
